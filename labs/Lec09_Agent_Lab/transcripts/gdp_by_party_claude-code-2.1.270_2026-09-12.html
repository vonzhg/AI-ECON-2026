Does the president's party move GDP growth?

Splitting the 26 years from 2000–2025 by which party held the presidency (crediting each calendar year to whoever was inaugurated that January) gives:

Avg. growth, Democratic years (n=13)
2.4%
Avg. growth, Republican years (n=13)
2.0%
Median growth, both parties
2.5%

The 0.4-point gap in the average looks like a Democratic edge, but the medians are identical. That gap is not a broad, consistent pattern — it's produced almost entirely by two outlier years: the +6.2% snapback in 2021 (Biden) after the COVID crash, and the −2.6% trough of the 2008–09 financial crisis, which is booked to 2009 (Obama's first year) even though the crisis began in 2007–08 under Bush. Pull those two years out and the party averages converge to essentially the same number.

Why the pattern is mostly noise, not policy

  • Recessions aren't partisan. All three downturns in this period — the 2001 dot-com bust, the 2008 financial crisis, and the 2020 COVID shock — began under Republican presidents, but that reflects when global/financial shocks happened to hit, not a Republican effect on growth. A random reshuffling of which party held office in those years would reshuffle the averages just as much.
  • First-year attribution is arbitrary. A president inherits the outgoing administration's budget, Fed policy stance, and business-cycle momentum for most of year one. Crediting 2009's collapse to Obama (in office 11 months of that year) or 2021's rebound to Biden is a common convention, but it's a labeling choice, not evidence of causation — shift the boundary by a year and the party averages move noticeably.
  • The president doesn't set fiscal or monetary policy alone. Congress controls taxes and spending, and growth-relevant policy often reflects which party controls the House/Senate, not just the White House. Interest-rate policy — arguably the biggest short-run growth lever — is set by the Fed, an independent body that spans party turnovers.
  • Growth is dominated by things no president controls: productivity trends, demographics, oil-price shocks, global financial contagion, a pandemic. These swamp any plausible policy effect on a 1–4 year horizon.
  • Small sample. 26 annual observations spanning only 3–4 complete business cycles is nowhere near enough to detect a genuine partisan growth effect even if one existed at a plausible size (academic studies, e.g. Blinder & Watson 2016, find a similar "Democratic edge" back to 1947 but attribute nearly all of it to luck — oil shocks, productivity swings, and consumer/business confidence — rather than to policy differences).

Bottom line

The data show essentially no reliable relationship between the president's party and real GDP growth over 2000–2025. What looks like a Democratic advantage in the mean evaporates once you use the median or reassign a couple of boundary years, and the underlying drivers of the growth swings — financial crises, a pandemic, Fed policy — aren't things a party affiliation causes.

Source: US Bureau of Economic Analysis, Real GDP percent change (annual), via FRED series A191RL1A225NBEA. 2025 reflects the latest available annual estimate.
